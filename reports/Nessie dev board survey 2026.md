# The plan holds, but swap the board

Nessie's architecture is right. No board small and cool enough to fit in a plush can run open-vocabulary speech-to-text in real time on a microcontroller in 2026, so wake word, voice detection and speech segmentation on the device with Whisper on a home server stays the correct split. The weak link is the specific board. The Waveshare ESP32-P4-WIFI6 has **one microphone and no battery circuit**, while several ESP32-S3 voice kits already ship with **two microphones, an amplifier and LiPo charging** on one PCB ([Waveshare](https://www.waveshare.com/esp32-p4-wifi6.htm), [CNX Software](https://www.cnx-software.com/2025/09/26/espressif-echoear-esp32-s3-voice-controlled-ai-chatbot-with-circular-touchscreen-and-mic-array/)). Measured on the criteria Nessie cares about (fit in a plush, battery, readiness out of the box, on-device speech, price, SDK maturity), the ranked shortlist is Espressif EchoEar, then Waveshare ESP32-S3-AUDIO-Board, then M5Stack CoreS3, with the incumbent P4-WIFI6 fourth and XIAO ESP32-S3 Sense fifth. The P4 silicon is still the strongest ESP-SR target on paper, but its extra compute adds headroom, not a new capability: neither the S3 nor the P4 has a published open-vocabulary STT that runs in real time. If on-device STT ever becomes a hard requirement, the only class that fits a plush's power and heat limits is the Axera AX630C (M5Stack LLM630 / Module LLM, Sipeed MaixCAM2). It runs Whisper-tiny on its NPU at about 0.5–1.5 W. Off-the-shelf tiny models, though, roughly double their error rate on children's speech, which is a further reason to keep transcription on the server ([M5Stack](https://docs.m5stack.com/en/stackflow/models/whisper-tiny), [arXiv 2406.10507](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.10507)).

## Every shipping AI toy streams audio; none transcribes on device

The commodity "AI toy board" ecosystem has settled on one design, and it is Nessie's design. The open-source xiaozhi-esp32 project (**31.5k stars, MIT, 138 board directories**, supporting ESP32-S3, C3, C5, C6 and P4) runs only wake word plus acoustic front end (AEC, noise suppression, VAD) on device and streams Opus audio over WebSocket or MQTT+UDP to a server that does ASR → LLM → TTS ([78/xiaozhi-esp32](https://github.com/78/xiaozhi-esp32)). Espressif's own toy reference designs (ESP-Spot, ESP-VoCat, EchoEar) say outright that "LLM processing still relies on cloud servers". The same documentation recommends capacitive touch (built into S3/P4) and a 6-axis IMU for plush interaction ([Espressif LLM Solution](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-techpedia/en/latest/esp-friends/solution-introduction/ai/llm-solution.html)). On the commercial side, Moxie sent audio to Google Cloud STT despite a $1,499 octa-core platform ([Mozilla](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/pl/privacynotincluded/moxie-robot/)). Curio's plush hides a removable cloud-connected "Voice Box" in its belly ([futurezone.at](https://futurezone.at/produkte/grimes-musikerin-elon-musk-ki-stofftier-grok-spielzeug-sprachsteuerung-curio/402711061)), and a teardown of China's Tom Cat AI companion found an Allwinner R128 Wi-Fi audio SoC with the LLM in the cloud ([Gelonghui](https://m.gelonghui.com/p/2056583)). No commercial plush found in this survey does STT on device. Nessie's rule that raw audio never leaves the home network is stricter than every product surveyed. That matters: the 2025–2026 incidents (the Bondu exposure of ~50,000 child transcripts, Senate probes of Miko, FoloToy and Curio) all involved cloud-side data ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/02/03/ai-toy-bondu-chat-data-exposure-hassan), [Yahoo/Nexstar](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/senators-sound-alarm-seek-answers-234728700.html)).

The gap between "can transcribe" and "can transcribe fast enough to talk to" is what settles the architecture. The best on-device open-vocabulary result on an ESP32 is the third-party Oído project on an ESP32-S3 N16R8. It reaches an impressive **3.3% WER on LibriSpeech test-clean**, but runs at **1.97x real time** and shows text **6.9–7.4 s after** a 3.7 s command ends; its authors write that it "is not real time yet" ([Oído wiki](https://github.com/lokutor-ai/oido/wiki)). Espressif's MultiNet7 runs on both S3 and P4, but it recognizes a fixed list of commands (200 on P4 per the P4 docs, "up to 300" per the component page), not open speech ([ESP-SR P4 command docs](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-sr/en/latest/esp32p4/speech_command_recognition/README.html), [espressif/esp-sr](https://github.com/espressif/esp-sr)). No public benchmark exists of Whisper, Moonshine or sherpa-onnx on an ESP32-S3 or P4. Commercial MCU voice stacks (Picovoice, Cyberon, Sensory, Edge Impulse) all stop at wake word and constrained commands or intent ([Picovoice Cheetah](https://picovoice.ai/docs/cheetah/), [Renesas/Cyberon](https://renesas.com/en/document/prb/cyberon-dspotternlu)).

The table below maps hardware tiers to the best on-device STT evidence found. "Plush fit" weighs power, heat and size against a small LiPo inside insulating fabric.

The table shows a hard step between Tier A and Tier D. whisper.cpp tiny on an RK3588 CPU runs at **RTF 0.10** ([Turing Pi](https://turingpi.com/whisper-cpp-piper-tts-arm64-turing-pi-rk3588/)), about 20x faster than Oído on the S3. Only the AX630C reaches Tier D speed inside a power budget a plush could carry. Applying M5Stack's per-step figures to an assumed ~20-token, 5 s child utterance gives roughly **0.9 s for Whisper-tiny and 1.7 s for Whisper-base**. That is an estimate, not a measurement. The LLM630 Compute Kit carries a Li-ion charger (AW32001) and a BQ27220 fuel gauge, gets Wi-Fi 6 from an onboard ESP32-C6, and drives an NS4150B amplifier. The microphone and speaker attach through a header, though; they are not on the board ([M5Stack LLM630 docs](https://docs.m5stack.com/en/core/LLM630%20Compute%20Kit)). Sipeed's MaixCAM2 puts two mics, a 1 W speaker amp, Wi-Fi 6 + BLE 5.4 and Li-ion charging on one AX630C board, but also a camera and touchscreen Nessie would pay for and not use ([Sipeed wiki](https://wiki.sipeed.com/hardware/en/maixcam/maixcam2.html)). Both are rated only **0–40 °C ambient**, a real limit inside fabric next to a child's body. No measured AX630C temperature or throttling data was found.

Children's speech is the deciding second factor. In zero-shot use, Whisper-tiny scores **20.6% WER on MyST versus 13.4% for Whisper-small**, and **53.8% versus 25.4% on OGI read speech** ([arXiv 2406.10507](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.10507)). Fine-tuning tiny.en on child speech brings MyST WER from 28.0% down to 15.9% ([arXiv 2507.14451](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.14451v1)). MyST covers ages 8–11, so younger children will score worse. Any on-device STT that fits a toy would therefore be a tiny-class model that needs custom fine-tuning, while the laptop server can run larger models that tolerate children's speech better. This strengthens the server-STT design on accuracy grounds, not only on cost.

## Ranked shortlist for Nessie's actual architecture

Criteria and weighting follow from the architecture. Because STT stays on the server and no plush-scale MCU board does real-time STT, "on-device speech" is scored as quality of the speech front end (wake word, AEC, VAD, command words), not as STT. Two-mic input with a dedicated ADC (ES7210) is weighted heavily: Espressif's AEC and beamforming need it, and barge-in ("Nessie, stop") is impossible without good echo cancellation while the speaker is playing ([ESP-SR AEC](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-sr/en/latest/esp32s3/acoustic_echo_cancellation/README.html)). Readiness means mics, amplifier and LiPo charging already on the PCB. Fit penalizes displays and cameras, which add size, cost and power with no use in a plush.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Espressif EchoEar** (ESP32-S3, 8 MB PSRAM) | ~$40 AliExpress / $55 Amazon | 2x LMA3729 mic array, ES7210 + ES8311 | NS4150B 3 W | Li-ion connector, TP4057 charger (250 mA), BQ27220 gauge | Wi-Fi 4 + BLE 5 | "TBD" |
| 2 | **Waveshare ESP32-S3-AUDIO-Board** (S3R8) | unverified | dual digital mics, ES7210 + ES8311 | onboard, model unstated | MX1.25 LiPo header, charger, battery switch | Wi-Fi 4 + BLE 5 | unverified |
| 3 | **M5Stack CoreS3** (S3, 8 MB quad PSRAM) | unverified | dual mics via ES7210 | AW88298 1 W | **500 mAh internal battery**, AXP2101 PMIC | Wi-Fi 4 + BLE 5 | 54 x 54 x 15.5 mm |
| 4 | **Waveshare ESP32-P4-WIFI6** (incumbent; P4NRW32X, 32 MB PSRAM) | $13.99–$69.99 by kit | **1 mic**, ES8311 | NS4150B 3 W | **none** | Wi-Fi 6 + BT 5 via ESP32-C6 (SDIO) | unverified |

Sources: EchoEar ([CNX Software](https://www.cnx-software.com/2025/09/26/espressif-echoear-esp32-s3-voice-controlled-ai-chatbot-with-circular-touchscreen-and-mic-array/)); S3-AUDIO-Board ([Waveshare wiki](https://www.waveshare.com/wiki/ESP32-S3-AUDIO-Board)); CoreS3 ([M5Stack docs](https://docs.m5stack.com/en/core/CoreS3)); P4-WIFI6 ([Waveshare](https://www.waveshare.com/esp32-p4-wifi6.htm), amp per saved schematic in `docs/hardware/esp32-p4.md`); XIAO Sense ([Seeed wiki](https://wiki.seeedstudio.com/xiao_esp32s3_getting_started/)).

**EchoEar ranks first** because it is the only board found that combines a dual-mic array with an ES7210 ADC (the AEC-capable front end), a 3 W amplifier, a charger *and* a fuel gauge, an IMU for pick-up and hug gestures, and Espressif first-party firmware (esp-brookesia) explicitly aimed at "smart toys". All of that comes at a verified ~$40 price ([CNX Software](https://www.cnx-software.com/2025/09/26/espressif-echoear-esp32-s3-voice-controlled-ai-chatbot-with-circular-touchscreen-and-mic-array/)). Its costs are a 1.85" round touchscreen Nessie does not need, an unstated battery capacity and board size, and a slow 250 mA charger. **The S3-AUDIO-Board ranks second.** It matches EchoEar's audio chain (ES7210 + ES8311, dual mics, battery charging with a hardware switch) without forcing a display (LCD and camera are connectors only), and Waveshare ships ESP-SR wake-word demos for it ([Waveshare wiki](https://www.waveshare.com/wiki/ESP32-S3-AUDIO-Board), [CNX Software](https://www.cnx-software.com/?p=156657)). It ranks below EchoEar only because its price, size and amplifier part could not be verified. **CoreS3 ranks third.** It is the most finished enclosed unit (battery included, PMIC, 54 mm square), but it carries a screen and camera, and its PSRAM is quad rather than the octal PSRAM ESP-SR recommends for the S3 ([esp-sr 1.6.0 readme](https://components.espressif.com/components/espressif/esp-sr/versions/1.6.0/readme)).

**The incumbent P4-WIFI6 ranks fourth despite having the best silicon.** ESP32-P4 is a first-class ESP-SR target: WakeNet10, VADNet, MultiNet7, and GSC beamforming/DOA as of ESP-SR 2.6.0 ([esp-sr 2.6.0](https://components.espressif.com/components/espressif/esp-sr/versions/2.6.0/readme)). Measured footprints are small. A single mic plus playback reference (the "MR" configuration) costs **60.1 KB RAM, 739.7 KB PSRAM and ~18.6% of one core**; dual-mic MMNR costs 79.1 KB / 1.15 MB / ~47% of one core ([ESP-SR P4 benchmark](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-sr/en/latest/esp32p4/benchmark/README.html)). At $13.99 for the bare board it is also the cheapest entry. Against the criteria, though, it scores worst on readiness: there is no battery circuit, so a LiPo charger must be added. The single onboard mic restricts the AFE to the M/MR configurations with no beamforming. The only community board support package for it has no ESP-SR example ([espp BSP](https://components.espressif.com/components/espp/esp32-p4-wifi6-dev-kit/versions/1.3.8/readme)). And because the P4 has no radio of its own, Wi-Fi runs through an ESP32-C6 over SDIO, which adds a second always-on chip to the power budget that no source quantifies. **XIAO S3 Sense ranks fifth.** Its tiny footprint, charging and published current measurements are attractive, but one mic and no amplifier mean adding an I2S amp, and AEC quality will be limited ([Seeed wiki](https://wiki.seeedstudio.com/xiao_esp32s3_getting_started/)).

Several candidates were excluded, each for a criterion-based reason. XMOS-equipped voice kits (Home Assistant Voice PE, Satellite1, ReSpeaker Lite/XVF3800) have strong front ends but are mains-powered designs: Voice PE needs 5 V/2 A and Satellite1 needs 9 V/3 A ([CNX](https://cnx-software.com/2024/12/20/59-voice-preview-edition-adds-an-offline-smart-speaker-to-your-home-assistant-server), [CNX](https://www.cnx-software.com/2025/05/16/satellite1-dev-kit-is-an-home-assistant-compatible-diy-voice-assistant-with-esp32-s3-module-xmos-xu316-audio-processor/)). MCU+NPU dev kits (STM32N6570-DK, Alif, i.MX RT700, Infineon PSoC Edge) are $91–$375 boards with displays and mostly no battery or Wi-Fi. Arduino Nicla Voice and the XIAO nRF54LM20 offer microwatt-to-milliamp wake-word listening (NDP120 under 280 µW, [New Electronics](https://newelectronics.co.uk/content/news/edge-ai-chip-company-syntiant-announces-support-for-google-keywords); nRF54LM20B Axon ~1.5 mA whole system, [Novel Bits](https://novelbits.io/nordic-axon-voice-workout-tracker/)) but are BLE-only. That makes them candidates for a later low-power wake co-processor, not the main board. ESP32-C3/C5 toy boards drop or weaken the wake word: FoloToy's C3 AI Passport sets `CONFIG_WAKE_WORD_DISABLED=y` ([xiaozhi folotoy](https://github.com/78/xiaozhi-esp32/tree/main/main/boards/folotoy/ai-passport)).

## Battery life and enclosure remain unmeasured everywhere

No vendor publishes battery life for continuous wake-word listening, so every figure here is built up from parts. The only board-level measurements are Seeed's: XIAO ESP32-S3 at **44 mA in modem-sleep, 110 mA with Wi-Fi active, 54.6 mA average while recording the mic** ([Seeed wiki](https://wiki.seeedstudio.com/xiao_esp32s3_getting_started/)). From those, an S3 listening with AFE + WakeNet and Wi-Fi in modem-sleep plausibly draws 50–80 mA, or roughly 12–20 hours on a 1000 mAh cell, with less during active conversation. This is an inference, not a measurement. The P4 + C6 pair has no board-level current data at all. The P4 datasheet does describe a **hardware VAD peripheral**, but no source shows ESP-SR using it; ESP-SR's VAD is VADNet, a neural network running in software ([ESP-SR VADNet](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-sr/en/latest/esp32p4/vadnet/README.html)). Nessie's CLAUDE.md should not assume the hardware VAD gives a power saving until that is shown. Battery safety is a gap in every hobby board: all surveyed kits use a bare 3.7 V LiPo on an MX1.25/JST header. The only commercial precedents for kids' audio devices are Toniebox choosing NiMH explicitly "as safer than lithium-ion" ([iFixit](https://ifixit.com/Teardown/Toniebox+Teardown/106148)) and Curio's removable, washable voice box. No source measured how much plush fabric and stuffing weaken the microphone or speaker.

## Conclusion

The survey changes what the P4 decision is about. The question is no longer which chip can do speech recognition, since none in the plush's power budget can. It is which board gets a dual-mic, battery-powered, echo-cancelling front end working soonest. On that question the S3 audio kits beat the P4-WIFI6 on every readiness criterion while running the same ESP-SR APIs. Two options follow, each with a clear tradeoff. Option one keeps the P4-WIFI6 and adds a LiPo charger and, ideally, a second mic. It keeps 32 MB PSRAM, Wi-Fi 6 and the most compute headroom, at the cost of hardware integration work and an unmeasured power overhead from the C6. Option two prototypes on EchoEar or the S3-AUDIO-Board. It gets dual-mic AEC and battery operation on day one, at the cost of Wi-Fi 4 and a smaller PSRAM margin. A 16 kHz, 16-bit mono stream is only 256 kbps, so Wi-Fi 6 adds little for this traffic. Measured against readiness, battery and front-end quality, option two is the logical conclusion. It costs little to move later: xiaozhi already carries board definitions for both S3 audio kits and P4 boards, so the firmware stays portable ([xiaozhi boards](https://github.com/78/xiaozhi-esp32/tree/main/main/boards)).

The AX630C is the realistic escape hatch if "raw audio never leaves the toy" ever replaces "never leaves the home". It is also the only plush-scale path to working offline when the laptop is away. Even then it would need fine-tuning on child speech before tiny-model accuracy is acceptable, so the most useful experiment now is to bench-measure listening current and AEC quality inside a stuffed toy. No published source has data on either.
